What it actually does

Every time any device looks up an internet address, it asks a “DNS server” — the internet's phone book. Normally that is your broadband provider. This box becomes your phone book instead.

📺

Your TV asks for an ad

“Where is ads.example-tracker.com?” — the same question an app or a web page asks dozens of times a minute.

🛡️

The box checks a list

Hundreds of thousands of known ad and tracker addresses, refreshed daily from public lists maintained by volunteers.

🚫

It answers “nowhere”

The ad is never fetched: it uses no bandwidth, it loads no tracker, and the page or app just carries on without it.

⚡

Everything else gets faster

Real addresses are answered from memory and refreshed before they go stale, so ordinary browsing feels a touch snappier.

What it does wellWhat it can't do
Banner ads, pop-ups, tracking pixels and telemetry on every device, with nothing installed on them. YouTube ads. They come from the same addresses as the videos, so no DNS filter can remove them.
Smart-TV and streaming-stick “phone home” traffic, which is where a lot of the creepiness lives. Ads baked into a video stream (Hulu, live TV). The ad is part of the picture.
Ads inside most mobile apps and games — often the single biggest win. Cosmetic tidy-up. A browser extension can hide the empty space; this can only stop the download.
Works on guest Wi-Fi, consoles, e-readers, anything that uses your router. Devices that ignore your router (some Chromecasts, phones with private DNS on). The guide covers how to force those too.
Typical result: 15–35% of all look-ups blocked, several hundred megabytes of ads not downloaded per week, and a noticeably quieter smart TV.
